# One question at a time

A dossier that only draws charts is written for someone who already knows what to look at. This section is the part that is not.

## The shape of a card

Four parts, and the fourth is the one that keeps this from becoming a scoring system.

| Part | Rule |
|---|---|
| **The question** | Plain language, no jargon, ends in a question mark. "Can it charge what it likes?" — not "Gross margin analysis" |
| **The figure** | The latest value, the median over the whole series, and a sparkline. The median matters more than the latest, and putting them side by side says so without an argument |
| **How to read it** | What the number means in words a first-time reader can follow, what a good and a bad shape look like, and which other card it should be read beside |
| **What it does not tell you** | The limit of the measure. Not optional |

Write the fourth part first if it helps. A metric with no stated limit reads as a verdict, and **the file does not deliver verdicts.**

## The ten

Each is computable from XBRL company facts alone. Where a threshold is quoted it carries its source, because a number someone else chose is not a fact about the business.

| Question | Figure | The point |
|---|---|---|
| Can it charge what it likes? | Gross margin | Level matters less than steadiness through a downturn |
| How much of a sale becomes profit? | Net margin | Buffett and Clark: **consistently above 20%** suggests a durable advantage, **consistently below 10%** a business where competition prevents one |
| Does the profit turn into cash? | CFO ÷ net income | Persistently below 100% has a specific cause every time |
| What could an owner take out? | **Owner earnings** | Net income + depreciation and other non-cash charges − the capital spending needed to hold position. Buffett's own measure, 1986 letter |
| What must it spend to stand still? | Capex ÷ CFO | Low and stable beside strong conversion is a business that funds itself |
| What does it earn on the owners' money? | Return on equity | Only meaningful read beside the debt card |
| Could debt sink it? | Interest ÷ operating income, debt ÷ equity | Buffett and Clark cite under 15% as comfortable for consumer businesses |
| Is your slice getting smaller? | Diluted share count over the full period | The only card measured for the reader rather than the company |
| Has it ever lost money? | Loss years out of total | What the loss years had in common is the question, not the count |
| How much of gross profit goes to research? | R&D ÷ gross profit | Read against the gross margin: heavy research with a rising margin is a different story from heavy research with a falling one |

## Owner earnings needs a caveat, every time

Buffett's definition subtracts **maintenance** capital spending — what the business needs to hold its position, as distinct from what it spends to grow. No company discloses that split.

The conservative substitute is to subtract **all** capital spending, which overstates the deduction for a growing company. That is the direction to err in, and the file says which it did.

It also means owner earnings only exists for the years capital expenditure is reported. On the worked NVIDIA dossier that is eight years of nineteen, and the gap is shown rather than filled.

## Rules for writing the cards

**No composite score.** Not a total, not a grade, not a count of how many "pass". A score invites the reader to stop thinking at the moment the thinking should start, and it hides which input drove it.

**No thresholds without a source.** "Above 20% is good" is someone's rule of thumb; say whose. A reader who knows the source can disagree with it, which is the point.

**The median before the latest.** One year is noise. Every card shows the median over the full series next to the current figure, and where a single year is unusual the card says so.

**Name the companion card.** Return on equity without the debt card is misleading; capex intensity without cash conversion is half a picture; research intensity without gross margin says nothing. Each card names the one it must be read beside.

**Charts get a line too.** Above each chart, one sentence on what to look for — the shape, not the last point. A chart caption that only restates the axis labels is wasted.

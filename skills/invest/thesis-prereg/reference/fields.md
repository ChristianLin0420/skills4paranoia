# The fields, and how each gets fudged

Every field here exists because a specific way of being wrong slips through without it. The
notes on fudging matter more than the definitions — a field filled in badly is worse than one
left blank, because it looks done.

## 1. The mispricing

Two questions, and the second is the one that gets skipped.

**Why is it cheap?** Earnings are depressed, the sector is out of favour, a segment is
misunderstood, it screens badly for a mechanical reason, a forced seller.

**Why does it stay cheap?** If the mispricing is obvious and nothing stops it closing, it
closes before you finish reading. A durable mispricing needs a reason it persists: an index
that cannot hold it, a disclosure that buries the good segment, a time horizon shorter than
the fix, a size below where the money is.

> **Fudged as:** "the market is being short-sighted." That is a description of every price
> you have ever disagreed with. Name the mechanism, and name who is on the other side of it.

## 2. What must be true

Two to four claims the thesis rests on. Each one load-bearing: if it fails, the thesis fails.

> **Fudged as:** a list of everything good about the company. Six or seven "musts" is not a
> more careful thesis, it is one that has not been reduced. If a claim can fail without the
> thesis failing, it is colour, not a claim.

## 3. A falsifier per claim

For each claim: **the observation that would show it false, and by when.**

An observation is something you could read off a filing, a print, a shipment number, a
competitor's disclosure. Not a feeling, not a price.

| Not a falsifier | A falsifier |
|---|---|
| "If growth slows" | "Revenue growth under 8% for two consecutive quarters" |
| "If margins come under pressure" | "Gross margin below 41% for a full year" |
| "If the story changes" | "The segment stops being broken out" |
| "If the stock keeps falling" | — a price is not evidence about a business |

> **Fudged as:** a falsifier so extreme it will never fire ("if they lose all their
> customers"). Test it by asking what the world looks like the day *before* it fires. If that
> world is already indistinguishable from disaster, the falsifier is decoration.

## 4. Horizon — and the rule

State the holding horizon. Then state, for each falsifier, **how long until it could be
observed**.

> **The falsification horizon must be shorter than the holding horizon.**

If it takes five years to know and you are holding three, the thesis cannot resolve inside
the time you are giving it, and the exit will happen on price or on fatigue. The fix is a
**nearer observable** — something that moves inside the horizon and correlates with the claim
— not a longer horizon.

Also state what *too early to tell* looks like, so it is distinguishable from *wrong*. Without
it, every disappointing quarter is "too early" until the position is closed for other reasons.

## 5. Exit, in both directions

**Down** — the thesis broke. Which falsifier, and what you do when it fires.

**Up** — the thesis played out. What has to be true for you to stop holding: the claims came
true and are now priced, the mispricing mechanism you identified has closed, the position
outgrew the job it was doing.

> **Fudged as:** leaving the up-exit blank because "why would I sell a winner". Most retail
> value investing has a buy discipline and no sell discipline, so positions are held until
> something goes wrong rather than until the reason for holding is gone. Those are different
> events and only one of them was ever in your thesis.

## 6. What would make you add

Pre-commit to it. Then adding to a working thesis and averaging into a broken one are
different acts on paper, at the moment you are least able to tell them apart.

> **Fudged as:** "if it gets cheaper." That is the definition of averaging down, stated as a
> plan.

## 7. The other side

Someone sold this to you at this price. Write their case at its strongest — not the weak
version you can dismiss.

> **Fudged as:** a strawman. If you cannot write the bear case in a form a bear would sign,
> you do not understand the position yet.

## 8. The outside view

What usually happens to situations of this type? Turnarounds that mostly do not turn,
serial acquirers, commodity producers at cycle peaks, companies whose fix requires a culture
change.

> **Fudged as:** skipping it, because the inside view is always more vivid. The base rate is
> the prior; your analysis is the update. A thesis that never mentions the base rate has
> quietly assumed it does not apply.

## 9. What this position is for

One line: the job it does in the portfolio. Keep it consistent with `allocation-jobs`, where
the job also gets a definition of failing at it.

> **Fudged as:** "it should go up." Everything is for that.

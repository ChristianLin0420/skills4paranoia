# Thesis — Aldergrove Industrial · frozen 2026-09-07

**In one sentence.** A small, fast-growing service segment is buried inside a declining
distribution business and is not broken out, so the whole company screens as a dying
distributor at 7× earnings; if the segment keeps compounding it crosses the disclosure
threshold in about two years and the market gets to see it.

**Holding horizon.** 3 years · **Longest falsification horizon.** 18 months ✓

**Job in the portfolio.** Idiosyncratic value — return that does not depend on the index.
Sized so that being wrong is survivable, not so that being right is decisive.

---

## The mispricing

**Why it is cheap.** Group revenue is 82% low-margin distribution, declining low single
digits. Group gross margin is 19% and falling. On any screen it is a melting ice cube, and
it is priced like one.

**Why it stays cheap.** *This is the load-bearing part.* The service segment is reported
inside "Industrial Solutions" and is never disclosed separately, so its growth and its
margin are invisible in every dataset a screen or a quant model uses. It is too small to
move the group margin yet, and management is not obliged to segment it until it crosses a
materiality threshold. Sell-side coverage is one analyst at a regional broker.

The other side of the trade is anyone who values the group off reported segments — which
is nearly everyone, because there is nothing else to value it off.

## What must be true

| # | Claim | Falsifier — a specific observation | Observable by |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The service segment is compounding above 25% a year | The MD&A's qualitative growth language falls to "modest" or a figure under 15%, in two consecutive half-year reports | 12 months |
| 2 | Distribution decline stays around −3% a year and is not accelerating | Distribution revenue falls more than 8% in any full year | 18 months |
| 3 | The segment gets broken out once it is material | It crosses 15% of group revenue and is still not disclosed separately in the following annual report | *see below* |

**Too early to tell looks like:** one soft half in the service segment with the group
margin flat. Two soft halves is claim 1 falsified, not a slow patch.

### Claim 3 failed the rule on the first pass

As first written, claim 3's falsifier could not be observed until the segment crossed 15%,
which is roughly 24 months out, and then only at the *following* annual report — 30 to 36
months. That is at or beyond the 3-year horizon: the thesis could not have been shown wrong
inside the time it was being given, and the exit would have happened on price or on fatigue.

The horizon was not extended. A nearer observable was substituted: **management's language
about segmentation in the next two annual reports.** If they are asked and decline twice
without giving a threshold, claim 3 is falsified at 18 months instead of 36.

Weaker evidence, and it resolves inside the horizon. That is the trade the rule forces.

## Exit

**Down.** Any falsifier fires → close. Not trim. The mispricing mechanism is a single
mechanism; if it is not there, the rest of the position is a declining distributor bought
at 7×, which was never the thesis.

**Up.** The segment is broken out and the group re-rates above 12× → close. The mispricing
I identified will have closed, and holding past that point is a different thesis about
business quality that this document does not make and has not tested.

**Add.** Only if service growth accelerates above 30% while the price is unchanged — the
thesis strengthening, not the price falling. A lower price on unchanged evidence is not a
reason to add and does not qualify.

## The other side

The service segment is small and its growth is off a base that flatters the rate. It sells
service attached to the distributed installed base, so it is not independent of the decline
— it is downstream of it, and it starts shrinking one replacement cycle later. Management
has an active reason *not* to segment: disclosed separately, it invites comparison with
pure-play service peers on capital intensity and win rate, where it would look worse. The
low multiple is not an oversight; it is a correct price for a business whose good part is
neither large, nor durable, nor going to be shown to you.

## The outside view

Sum-of-the-parts theses that depend on management choosing to disclose or separate usually
fail, and they fail slowly, which is the expensive way. The common outcome is not that the
analysis was wrong about the segment — it is that the segment was right and nothing ever
forced the market to look. **The base rate for "good business hidden inside a bad one" is
value trap**, and this thesis needs a specific reason it is not, which is why claim 3 is a
claim and not an assumption.
